## Honest Assessment After One Year of Chinese

---

### Overall Level

You are approximately **HSK 2, with some HSK 3 vocabulary exposure**. For one year of study, that is a reasonable but not exceptional position. Chinese is consistently ranked among the hardest languages for English speakers, so context matters. That said, one year should yield somewhat more structural fluency than what is visible in this transcript.

---

### What You Are Doing Well

**Comprehension is your strongest skill.** You understand a native speaker conducting an entire lesson in Chinese. You catch corrections, follow explanations, and respond appropriately. That is genuinely non-trivial at one year. Many learners at your stage still struggle to follow a native speaker at natural speed.

**You communicate.** Your ideas get across. When you wanted to explain financialization, the concept landed even through broken sentences. Communication, however imperfect, is the point. You are not paralyzed by fear of speaking.

**You are accumulating real vocabulary.** Words like 同父异母, 业绩, 销售额, 影响力, and 国内生产总值 are not beginner words. You are being exposed to and retaining substantive content.

---

### Where You Are Genuinely Weak

These are the honest problem areas, ranked by severity.

---

**1. Your sentence production is severely fragmented.**

This is the most significant issue. Almost every sentence you produce in the transcript is incomplete, restarted multiple times, or abandoned mid-way. For example:

> 呃，在-在，我觉得在美国，呃，我们没有很多公司，呃，有，呃，在-在英文我们说 financialized...

This is not just the natural hesitation of a language learner. It reflects that you do not yet have stable sentence templates in your head. You are constructing grammar from scratch every time instead of reaching for learned patterns. After one year, you should have a small set of sentence frames that come out automatically and reliably.

---

**2. You switch to English far too often and too early.**

The English filler words — "yeah," "OK," "like," "one second" — appear constantly and function as a crutch that lets you avoid fully committing to Chinese. More importantly, whenever a thought gets complex, you exit into English rather than simplifying the thought to fit your current Chinese ability. A more productive habit is to simplify your idea and express it in Chinese you already control, rather than reaching for English.

---

**3. Your basic vocabulary gaps are larger than expected for one year.**

In this lesson you did not know: 蔬菜 (vegetables), 冰箱 (refrigerator), 微波炉 (microwave), 名词 (noun), 动词 (verb). These are not advanced words. 蔬菜 and 冰箱 in particular appear in most HSK 2 lists. This suggests your vocabulary study may be uneven — you have been exposed to sophisticated words through your class content, but some common everyday words have been missed.

---

**4. Core grammar particles are unstable.**

You consistently drop or misplace 了, confuse 那 and 哪, and misuse 的. These are not occasional slips. They appear repeatedly across the transcript. After one year, 了 for completed actions, 哪 for questions, and basic 的 placement should be approaching automatic. They are not yet.

---

**5. You confuse words you have learned.**

You said 华北 when you meant 华东, used 打 instead of 太 in 不太好, and at one point stated that 增加 (increase) is bad, when the entire lesson context was about salary increases being good. This suggests that vocabulary is being stored loosely rather than anchored to clear meaning. You are recognizing words without fully owning them.

---

**6. The conversation is almost entirely teacher-led.**

The teacher asks a question, you attempt an answer. You very rarely initiate a topic, ask the teacher something in Chinese, or extend a conversation thread unprompted. This is a passive conversational posture. It limits how much real production practice you are getting even within the lesson itself.

---

### The Core Issue

You are learning a lot of content but your foundational spoken output has not kept pace. You can recognize and understand far more than you can produce cleanly. This gap is common, but if it is not addressed it tends to widen over time rather than close.

---

### What This Means Practically

After one year, a learner with strong fundamentals should be able to produce short, complete, grammatically reliable sentences on familiar topics without restarting. You are not consistently doing that yet. Your comprehension is ahead of your output, your vocabulary exposure is ahead of your grammatical control, and your receptive skills are ahead of your productive ones across the board.

This is not unusual, and it is not cause for alarm. But if you continue for another year with the same approach, you risk reinforcing broken output patterns rather than correcting them.

---

### What Would Actually Help

- **Drill sentence patterns until they are automatic**, not just understood. Pick five structures from this lesson and practice producing them with different content every day.
- **Stop switching to English when a thought gets hard.** Simplify the thought instead. Say something simpler but complete, rather than something complex but broken.
- **Fill the everyday vocabulary gaps.** Use a spaced repetition system and make sure HSK 2 core words are solidly known before accumulating more advanced terms.
- **Record yourself speaking for 2 minutes daily.** Listen back. You will hear the fragments and restarts in a way that is harder to notice in the moment.

---

One year of Chinese is genuinely not very long. The foundation is there. But the honest answer to your question is that your output is below where your comprehension and vocabulary exposure suggest you should be, and that gap is the thing worth focusing on now.
